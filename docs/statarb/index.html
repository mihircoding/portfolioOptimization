Statistical arbitrage across 4,950 pairs

Pairs Trading Lab

A cointegration screen run across every pair in the S&P 100, then traded on data it was never selected on. 930 of 4,950 pairs passed the statistical test. Out of sample they averaged a Sharpe ratio of −0.009.

Method

How it was tested

Pairs trading bets that two related securities which drift apart will converge. The hard part is establishing that the relationship is real and not an artifact of the sample.

The pipeline

  • Hedge ratio — OLS of one leg on the other; the slope is how many shares hedge one share.
  • Engle-Granger test — ADF on the regression residual. A low p-value rejects "this spread is a random walk".
  • Scan — all 4,950 unordered pairs, ranked by p-value.
  • Signal — rolling z-score, with a state machine for entry/exit hysteresis.
  • Backtest — equal-dollar legs, costs on every position change.

Controls

  • No lookahead — the z-score uses a rolling window that ends at t, never the full-sample mean. Verified: truncating the series leaves earlier values bit-identical.
  • Positions lag by one bar — positions.shift(1). You decide at today's close and earn tomorrow's return.
  • Selection and testing are separated — pairs and betas come from the formation window; performance is measured only on the trading window.

Multiple comparisons is the whole story. Running 4,950 tests at a 5% threshold means roughly 248 pairs pass on luck alone, before any real relationship exists. Correcting for that — Bonferroni, p ≤ 0.05/4,950 = 1.01×10−5 — leaves exactly one pair standing.

930 passed, which is more than 248. That gap is not 682 discoveries: large-cap equities share market-wide factors, so many pairs co-move enough to fool a test built on an independence assumption.

Where the p-values landed

Under a true null these would be flat across [0,1]. The pile-up near zero is partly genuine co-movement and partly the ADF test being too permissive when beta is estimated rather than known.

Control group

Now trade the 4,020 pairs the screen threw away

Every number on this page so far describes the 930 pairs that passed. Compared to what? A screen that keeps 930 of 4,950 is only worth running if the 4,020 it rejected would have done worse — and nobody checks, because the rejects are by definition the ones you don't trade. control.py trades them anyway, under identical rules. The only difference between the two groups is which side of p = 0.05 they landed on.

The screen itself

Is the screen’s 5% really 5%?

Everything above takes the cointegration screen’s arithmetic at face value and shows the arithmetic doesn’t pay. This checks the arithmetic. screen_validity.py regenerates every number here.

The screen does what nearly every pairs-trading tutorial does: regress one log price on the other, take the residual, run an augmented Dickey–Fuller test on it. The residual is not data. It is the output of a regression chosen to make it as small as possible, so it looks more stationary than a series of the same character would — the fit has already spent some of the wandering. Dickey–Fuller’s tables were computed for a series nobody fitted anything to. That is why Engle–Granger and Phillips–Ouliaris published separate critical values, and why statsmodels ships coint() next to adfuller().

The only definition of a test’s size that needs no theory: feed it — pairs of independent random walks, where there is nothing to find by construction, and count how often it says there is. A test labelled 5% fires — of the time.

If the screen were correctly sized these would lie on top of each other. The naive version piles up against zero; the corrected one is much closer to the flat line a mostly-null universe should produce.

This corrects a number stated further up this page. The walk-forward section reads 651–1,555 passes per quarter against “the ~248 that pure chance predicts” as evidence of genuine structure. That 248 is 4,950 × 5% — the nominal size. The measured size is —, so chance predicts about — passes a quarter, and the median quarter’s 799 is barely above it. The claim that 100 large-cap US stocks share real structure is still true; the claim that the pass counts were evidence of it is not. The paragraph is left standing with this correction attached rather than quietly edited.

And fixing the statistics does not rescue the strategy

GroupPairsMean SharpeBook Sharpe

The tempting conclusion is that the screen was reading the wrong table and the survivors are the real pairs. The seventeen best-evidenced pairs in the universe trade no better than the 4,020 the screen threw away. The screen had two separate problems — it was miscalibrated by a factor of three, and the property it tests for does not survive into the next five years at a strength worth trading. Fixing the first does not touch the second.

Walk-forward

What if you re-estimated every quarter?

Everything above picks pairs and hedge ratios once, in 2020, and then trades five years without looking again. Nobody runs a pairs book that way, so this is the first objection to the whole study. walkforward.py removes it: every quarter, re-scan all 4,950 pairs on the trailing three years, keep what passes, re-estimate β on the same window, and trade that book for one quarter.

What gets re-estimated

"Pairs" is the average number held per quarter; "churn" is the share of the roster that changes at each re-estimate. Tightening the screen to 1% cuts the book by two thirds and the return comes out at +0.06%. Sizing the z-score window from each pair's own half-life gives −0.02%.

Refitting doesn't rescue it, and it barely matters at all. Refitting β, re-running the screen, tightening it, and changing the signal window are five different answers to "what should be re-estimated", and all five land within 1.3 points of zero over five years. A parameter that is genuinely on the wrong setting shows up as a result that changes when you change it. This one stays at zero whichever way it is refit.

How many of the 20 quarters each pair passed

—

Not one pair out of 4,950 is cointegrated in all twenty quarters. Cointegration is supposed to be a property of a relationship, not of a window. Two stocks that are genuinely tied together, with the same business, inputs and customers, don't come untied for nine months and then tie back up. A screen whose verdict holds one quarter in five is measuring which noise happened to look like reversion in the last three years, and re-running it every quarter draws a fresh sample of that noise.

This is the p-value bucket result from the other side. That one showed the screen can't rank pairs; this one shows it can't repeat. The structure in the universe is real, but it isn't stable at the level of a pair, on this horizon, at a strength this test can find.

Limitations

Biases and modelling choices

Known biases

  • Survivorship — this is the S&P 100 as it stands today. Membership is awarded for having already grown large, so testing from 2013 asks how today's winners behaved on the way to winning. Firms that collapsed or were acquired are absent. Fixing it needs a point-in-time constituent list (CRSP, Compustat).
  • Parameter selection — the 60-day window and 2.0/0.5 thresholds were chosen by convention, not fitted, but they were chosen by someone who knows how this period played out.
  • Costs are optimistic — 5bps ignores short-borrow cost on one leg, a continuous drag on any market-neutral book.

Modelling choices

  • Equal dollar legs, not beta-weighted — β defines the spread the signal is built on, but weighting positions by it needs daily rebalancing whose weights drift and whose trades cost money.
  • ADF is too permissive here — because β is estimated, the residual is already optimised to look stationary. statsmodels.coint() uses stricter Engle-Granger critical values and consistently returns larger p-values.
  • Lag search capped at 12 — uncapped, AIC considers ~23 lags for this sample. Capping cut cost per test from 45ms to 11ms with near-identical answers.
