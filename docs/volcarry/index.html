The model

What the formula actually says

Black-Scholes prices a European option under the assumption that the stock follows a random walk with constant volatility. The whole thing hangs on two numbers:

d1 = [ln(S/K) + (r − q + σ²/2)T] / (σ√T)
d2 = d1 − σ√T

call = S·e−qTN(d1) − K·e−rTN(d2)
put  = K·e−rTN(−d2) − S·e−qTN(−d1)

N(d2) is (roughly) the risk-neutral probability the option finishes in the money; N(d1) weights that probability by how big the payoff is when it happens. Read the call formula as "what you get, minus what you pay, both discounted."

Dividends aren't cosmetic

Holding an option doesn't entitle you to dividends, and the stock drops by the dividend on each ex-date. Merton's fix is to discount the spot by e−qT everywhere it appears — you're effectively optioning the stock minus the dividends you won't receive.

Dividends drag the forward price down, so they make calls cheaper and puts dearer. Move the dividend slider above and watch both prices go opposite directions. The test suite checks the dividend-adjusted parity identity C − P = Se−qT − Ke−rT holds to 1e-9.

Implied volatility goes the other way

Given a market price, find the σ that reproduces it. There's no closed form, so the solver bisects: price is monotonically increasing in volatility, so keep halving [0.0001, 5.0] until the model price matches. That's what turns the market's quotes into the volatility smile.

Cross-checking

Why price the same thing three times

Because agreement between methods that share an assumption but nothing else is the only evidence you have that the implementation is right. A closed form can be algebraically wrong in a way that still looks plausible; it cannot be wrong in a way that a simulation of the same process happens to reproduce.

Monte Carlo

Skip the calculus. Draw thousands of terminal prices straight from the risk-neutral distribution, average the payoffs, discount:

S_T = S·exp((r − q − σ²/2)T + σ√T·Z)

It reports a standard error alongside the estimate, because it's an estimate — the test suite requires it to land within 4 standard errors of the closed form at 500k paths, with and without dividends. Convergence is O(1/√n): 100× more paths buys one extra digit.

Binomial tree — and what it can do that the others can't

Black-Scholes and Monte Carlo both price European exercise only. The model cannot ask "what if I exercised early?" because the derivation assumes you can't. Most listed US equity options are American.

A Cox-Ross-Rubinstein tree can: at every node, walking backward from expiry, it compares holding against exercising immediately and keeps whichever is worth more. Three results worth knowing, all checked directly in the test suite rather than assumed:

  • With no dividend, an American call is worth exactly the same as its European twin — exercising early throws away time value for nothing.
  • A deep in-the-money American put is worth more even with no dividend: locking in the strike starts earning interest sooner.
  • Add a dividend and American calls pick up a premium too — now there's something to capture.

Greeks by simulation

Two techniques, because one can't do all three

Delta and vega use pathwise differentiation. The simulated terminal price is a smooth function of both spot and volatility — dS_T/dS = S_T/S — so the derivative can be pushed inside the expectation and estimated from the very same paths that priced the option.

Gamma can't use that trick at all. It needs the derivative of the payoff's slope, and a call's slope jumps from 0 to 1 at the strike. Differentiate that kink and you get a Dirac delta, not a number. This isn't a bug to work around — it's a structural limit of the pathwise method for any Greek involving a second derivative of a kinked payoff.

So gamma uses the likelihood-ratio (score function) method instead, which differentiates the probability density of the terminal price rather than the payoff. The density stays perfectly smooth even where the payoff doesn't:

gamma = e−rT/(S²σ²T) · E[payoff · (Z² − 1 − Zσ√T)]

The weight doesn't depend on the payoff being differentiable at all — it would work identically on a digital option's genuinely discontinuous payoff, which is exactly the case pathwise differentiation cannot handle even in principle. All three are checked against the closed-form Greeks within 4 standard errors.

The risk reversal and butterfly are read off the quoted deltas by interpolation, so "25-delta" means the strike the market treats as 25-delta — not one computed from a volatility we assumed.

Where the forward comes from, since it decides everything else. Black-Scholes needs a forward, and a forward needs a dividend yield and a borrow rate nobody publishes. The options quote both already: C − P = e−rT(F − K) is a straight line in K, so regressing call-minus-put on strike gives the discount factor as the slope and the forward from the intercept. The fit's R² comes back with the answer and a poor one gets the chain refused rather than quietly used.

The fix is inverting the binomial tree instead, at roughly 500× the cost per quote.

Sanity

Is the chain pricing a real distribution?

The smile above is built from quotes, and quotes are not always consistent with each other. Three conditions on the call price follow from the payoff alone — no model needed: it falls as the strike rises, a call spread never costs more than it can pay, and it is convex in the strike. The third is the one worth having, because the second derivative of the call price is the risk-neutral probability distribution.

The distribution SPY's option market is quoting for — (— days), read straight off the curvature of the call prices — Breeden–Litzenberger, 1978. No model was fitted to produce it. The dip below zero on the left is not a market view; it is two quotes that weren't alive at the same instant.

A butterfly quoted at a negative price is the market assigning negative probability to a range of prices, which never actually happens. What happened is that three quotes weren't simultaneous, or one leg is stale, or the forward is slightly off.

Density mass is the implied distribution integrated over the strikes the market quotes. It is — at a week and — at two years: the far-dated chain simply doesn't quote enough of the tails.

That matters more than it sounds. Anything computed from the far chain — an expected value, a tail probability, a risk number — is missing a seventh of its probability, and would come out looking like a confident figure if nobody checked. The check costs one pass over the quotes.

Filling the gaps between quoted strikes doesn't help either. Interpolating total variance and interpolating volatility produce almost the same number of bad butterflies (— on a 200-strike grid), because a straight line between two quoted IVs inherits whatever non-convexity the quotes already had. A surface you can price a book with has to be fitted under the convexity constraint, not joined up dot to dot — which is the next section.

The fix

Fit it, don't join the dots

The section above ends on a promise: a surface you can price a book with has to be fitted under the convexity constraint. svi.py is that fit. For log-moneyness k = log(K/F) it models total variance with Gatheral's raw SVI.

w(k) = a + b · ( ρ(k − m) + √((k − m)² + σ²) )

Five parameters, each one a feature you can point at: a the level, b the wing slope, ρ the tilt (negative for an equity index, because the crash is on the put side), m where the minimum sits, σ how rounded the bottom is. The reason for this function rather than a spline is its shape. It is a hyperbola, so it is convex in k by construction and goes linear in both wings — exactly what the no-arbitrage conditions ask for. A cubic spline has no such shape and will turn over in the wings however well it fits the quoted points.

The constraint is enforced during the fit, not checked afterwards. Gatheral and Jacquier give the risk-neutral density's sign in closed form from the parameters — g(k), implemented as durrleman_g — so a candidate whose density would go negative is rejected before it is ever accepted. That is the whole difference from interpolating: the surface cannot price a negative probability, for a structural reason rather than a lucky one.

And the five-parameter fit is really a two-parameter search. For fixed (m, σ) the model is linear in the other three, so those come out of a small weighted least-squares solve and only two parameters get searched — a grid, then a local refine. SVI has well-documented local minima; reducing the non-convex part to two dimensions removes that failure mode for about a thousand tiny solves, which is microseconds.

Fit error and “worst” are in volatility points, so 0.30 means three tenths of a vol away from the quote. “min g” is the smallest value of the density-sign function across the fitted range; positive everywhere means no butterfly arbitrage exists anywhere on the curve, including between the strikes and outside them.

The honest half, and it is not flattering. A single five-parameter function cannot reprice a liquid SPY chain inside its own bid-ask spreads. At one week it lands between the bid and the ask on a handful of strikes out of a hundred, and the worst miss is tens of half-spreads. At two years it is nearly all of them — not because the fit got better (the error in vol points is larger there) but because the spreads got wide enough to hide it.

That is the real trade being made, and it is worth saying rather than quoting an r-squared: SVI is a no-arbitrage interpolator, not a repricer. Its job is to give a usable surface at every strike nobody quoted, and the price of guaranteeing no arbitrage everywhere is that it will not match the busiest strikes to the penny. A desk that needs both runs SVI for the surface and carries a per-strike residual on top of it.

What the vega weighting buys

Worst repricing error, measured in half-spreads of that strike. Lower is better.

Each quote is weighted by its vega — how much the option's price actually responds to volatility. Equal weighting wins on root-mean-square error and loses badly on the thing that matters, by a factor of three to five on the worst repricing error.

The reason is worth keeping: a deep out-of-the-money option barely responds to volatility at all, so inverting its price for an implied vol produces a number that is mostly rounding. Equal weighting spends its accuracy on those quotes and pays for it at the strikes anyone trades. Fitting in the right units is a smaller decision than choosing the parameterization and a larger one than it looks.

One condition this does not touch: each expiry is fitted on its own, so nothing here prevents calendar arbitrage between expiries. That needs the fits tied together across maturity, and is the next thing to build.

Hedging

Running the hedge the price depends on

Every price on this page rests on one claim inside the derivation: that the option can be replicated by trading the stock, so it's worth exactly what the replication costs. Nobody trades continuously. hedging.py runs the replication for real, in discrete steps, and hedging_study.py runs it on SPY: sell a one-month at-the-money call on the first trading day of every month, at whatever VIX said, and delta-hedge it daily to expiry. — non-overlapping trades, —, all quoted per $100 of SPY so 2008 and 2024 are comparable.

Limitations

Where this model stops being true

  • Constant volatility is the assumption that breaks worst. The market prices each strike with its own implied vol — the smile — because real returns jump and have fat tails. The live app's market page displays that violation directly against real option chains.
  • European exercise in the closed form. US single-stock options are American, so deep ITM puts on dividend payers show the largest model-vs-market gaps.
  • lastPrice on illiquid strikes can be hours stale — check volume before concluding an option is mispriced.
  • "My model says it's cheap" is not a trade. The gap is mostly the smile, possibly American exercise premium, possibly a stale quote. The model is a lens for reading the market's expectations, not an alpha signal.
