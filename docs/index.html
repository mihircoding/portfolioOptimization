The optimizer lost to dividing by five

Portfolio Optimization

Markowitz mean-variance optimization, the efficient frontier, covariance shrinkage, risk parity and CVaR — implemented, then tested the only way that matters: out of sample. In sample the max-Sharpe portfolio wins with a Sharpe of 0.90. Walk it forward and it finishes fourth of six, behind equal weighting — which requires no estimation, no optimizer, and no trading at all.

Portfolio volatility is not a weighted average

Given weights w, expected returns μ and covariance Σ, the portfolio's variance is w′Σw — which is lower than the weighted average of the individual volatilities whenever assets are less than perfectly correlated. That gap is the whole idea.

Markowitz's contribution was making it quantitative: an asset is not risky or safe on its own, only in the context of what it's held alongside. A volatile asset that zigs when the rest of your book zags reduces portfolio risk.

Three portfolios fall out of it. Minimum variance minimizes w′Σw — note what's missing: no μ, so it needs no return forecasts at all. Maximum Sharpe maximizes return per unit of risk. The frontier is every risk level in between.

Two things are worth noticing anyway: both optimized portfolios zero out EFA and VNQ entirely (mean-variance doesn't hedge its bets, it picks), and min-variance puts 91% in a single asset — a portfolio built by a diversification algorithm that is barely diversified, because minimizing variance with no return constraint just finds the calmest thing available.

Risk

Weights lie. Risk contributions don't.

Asset i's share of total portfolio variance is w · (Σw) / (w′Σw). The decomposition is exact, not approximate — portfolio variance is homogeneous of degree 2 in the weights, so Euler's theorem makes the parts sum to the whole with nothing left over.

Equal weight: 20% of the capital, 1% of the risk

The portfolio that looks most balanced is, in risk terms, a leveraged bet on real estate and equities with a rounding error in bonds. This is the single most useful number in portfolio reporting, and it's why "asset X is 4% of the book and 38% of the risk" is a sentence risk managers say constantly.

Equal risk contribution closes that gap

ERC solves for the weights where every asset contributes equally to risk. There's no closed form except when all correlations are equal (where it collapses to inverse-vol), so it's solved numerically — started from inverse-vol weights, because the objective isn't convex and a good starting point keeps the solver out of trouble.

Like min-variance, ERC never sees expected returns. Same robustness argument, taken one step further: an optimizer cannot be wrecked by an input it never receives.

Why that matters so much here

Mean-variance treats μ and Σ as known constants. They're noisy estimates, and the optimizer's response to noise is pathological: it loads into whatever asset had the best estimation luck, because from inside the objective function a spuriously high mean is indistinguishable from a real edge. Michaud called it the error-maximizing property, and it's earned.

The asymmetry that makes it bite: expected returns are far harder to estimate than covariances. You need decades of data to pin down a mean return; a covariance converges in months. The input the optimizer is most sensitive to is the one you know least about.

The result that matters

The portfolio that required no estimation, no optimizer, no covariance matrix and no trading beat the one that used all four. This is DeMiguel, Garlappi & Uppal (2009) reproduced on a different universe and a later sample.

Turnover is the mechanism made visible: max-Sharpe rewrote 16.2% of the book every year, chasing an optimum that moved because the estimates moved, not because the world did. Note carefully that turnover is not the explanation for the performance gap — at realistic ETF costs, 16% one-way turnover is a couple of basis points a year. The gap is estimation error. The turnover is a symptom of it, not the cause.

Change the estimation window above and the ranking barely moves. Equal weight wins at 2, 3, 5 and 7 years; max-Sharpe is fourth at three of the four. That stability is what turns one result into a finding — a conclusion that flipped between a 3-year and a 5-year lookback would be a statement about the lookback, not about the methods.

And notice that more data made everything worse. Going from 2 to 7 years of estimation degraded every single method. That's the mean-return estimation problem in one row: a longer window buys precision about a parameter that isn't stable, so you estimate a stale quantity more accurately.

Tail risk

Variance is symmetric. Losses aren't.

Every method above optimizes variance, which penalizes a surprise gain exactly as much as a surprise loss of the same size. CVaR — the average loss in the worst 5% of days — only looks at the side you actually mind.

PortfolioVolVaR 95%CVaR 95%

Daily VaR is the loss exceeded one day in twenty; CVaR is the average loss on those days specifically.

Minimizing CVaR directly can't be done by sorting scenarios and averaging the tail — sorting isn't differentiable, and which scenarios are the worst changes discontinuously as the weights move. Rockafellar & Uryasev's result is that it's exactly equivalent to a linear program in an expanded variable space, which is what the project implements.

It's the only optimizer here that works from the actual historical scenarios rather than reducing them to (μ, Σ) first — so it can tell apart two assets with identical mean and variance but different tail shape, something every other method here is mathematically blind to. The test suite proves that on synthetic data built exactly that way.

And on this dataset it barely changes anything. Min CVaR converges to almost the same portfolio as min-variance — same weights to a percentage point, same out-of-sample Sharpe within noise. That's a finding about this universe, not a failure of the method: the asset both methods pile into (AGG) has both the lowest variance and unremarkable tail behaviour for its variance. When the calmest asset isn't also the crash-prone one, minimizing variance and minimizing tail risk point at the same corner.

Black-Litterman

A better prior, not a better forecast

Every complaint on this page comes back to μ. Black-Litterman runs mean-variance backwards to get one without a sample mean: assume the market portfolio is optimal for somebody, and exactly one return vector Π = δΣwmkt would have produced it. Then blend in your views, weighted by how sure you are of each.

The left column comes from the covariance matrix and observable fund sizes. The right column is eighteen years of whatever happened. Max-Sharpe optimizes against the right one.

Equilibrium says gold and bonds should return almost nothing, because they carry almost none of the market's risk. The sample says gold returned 9% a year. Both statements are true about different questions, and only one of them is an input you can estimate reliably.

The other thing the construction buys is coherence: a view about one asset propagates through Σ to everything correlated with it. Say gold does well and the posterior raises bonds too, without anyone writing a view about bonds. Plain max-Sharpe has no such mechanism — it only knows what you typed in.

In the walk-forward table above, Black-Litterman finishes first and second at every estimation window. That result is worth less than it looks, for the reason below.

Most of the edge was the anchor. Swapping today's fund sizes for a plausible outcome-blind global allocation costs 0.09 of Sharpe. Going all the way to an equal anchor lands right on top of equal weight — which is exactly what should happen, since Black-Litterman with an equal anchor and no views is equal weight with a covariance-shaped nudge. The method inherits whatever the anchor had; it doesn't manufacture an edge.

The views did essentially nothing. The momentum view adds 0.02 of Sharpe at two anchors and subtracts 0.04 at the third, while costing the highest turnover of anything in this study — more than max-Sharpe. Paying the most turnover on the page to buy 0.02 of Sharpe that flips sign under a different starting point is noise with a transaction cost, not a strategy.

Limitations

Limitations

The sample favours equal weight. 2010–2024 was a strong equity decade with weak bond returns, and equal weight carries the most equity risk of any method here — it was structurally positioned to win this particular sample. The finding "estimation error degrades optimization out of sample" is robust and well documented; the specific finding "equal weight beats everything" is partly this sample.

Sharpe here is computed on 15 annual returns, not daily. The standard error on any of these ratios is large, and the gap between 0.82 and 0.75 is not statistically significant on its own. What carries weight is the direction and its consistency across four independent window choices.

Risk-free rate is zero throughout. Over this period that flatters every portfolio equally, so it doesn't affect the ranking — but the Sharpe levels are overstated.

Turnover is measured, not charged. Adding realistic costs would widen the gap slightly in equal weight's favour and change nothing material.

Five assets is an easy problem. A sample covariance over N assets estimates N(N+1)/2 parameters — 15 here, but 1,275 for 50 assets. The estimation pathology gets dramatically worse with more assets, which is exactly where shrinkage, factor models and Black-Litterman earn their keep.
